Cognitive Shift 2 · Ownership Over Activity

From "Who is doing this?"
to "Who owns this?"

Rate each statement based on your organization's current reality — not aspiration.

What this shift measures Task thinkers assign work. Project Thinkers assign ownership — a named individual who is accountable for the outcome, not just the activity. Shared ownership in a task-thinking organization is the organizational equivalent of no ownership.
Question 6 of 25
Every active organizational initiative has a single named owner — not a team, not a department, but one individual who is accountable for the outcome and can be asked to report on it at any time.
Strongly disagree
Strongly agree
Question 7 of 25
In our organization, there is a clear distinction between being responsible for doing work and being accountable for the outcome — and team members understand which role they play on each initiative.
Strongly disagree
Strongly agree
Question 8 of 25
When a project stalls or misses a milestone in our organization, there is a clear owner who surfaces the problem, proposes a resolution, and drives the recovery — not a committee that discusses it.
Strongly disagree
Strongly agree
Question 9 of 25
Our leaders and managers are genuinely empowered to make decisions within their area of ownership — they do not need to escalate routine project choices to a higher authority to proceed.
Strongly disagree
Strongly agree
Question 10 of 25
Our organization has a standard RACI or equivalent ownership framework that is applied to every significant initiative — defining who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed — and it is followed in practice, not just in documentation.
Strongly disagree
Strongly agree

Cognitive Shift 4 · Learning Over Blame

From "What went wrong?"
to "What does this teach us?"

Rate each statement based on your organization's current reality — not aspiration.

What this shift measures Task thinkers conduct post-mortems focused on fault. Project Thinkers conduct learning cycles focused on improvement. This shift converts organizational failure from a source of blame into a source of competitive advantage — turning every setback into institutional knowledge.
Question 16 of 25
Our organization conducts structured retrospectives or lessons-learned reviews after every significant initiative — and the insights from those reviews are documented and consulted before similar projects begin.
Strongly disagree
Strongly agree
Question 17 of 25
When a project misses a milestone or fails to deliver its intended outcome, our organization's first response is to identify the systemic or process cause — not to identify who is at fault.
Strongly disagree
Strongly agree
Question 18 of 25
Team members in our organization feel psychologically safe surfacing problems, blockers, and risks to leadership — they do not wait until issues escalate because they fear how the information will be received.
Strongly disagree
Strongly agree
Question 19 of 25
Our organization has a visible, accessible repository of lessons learned from past initiatives — and new project teams are expected to review relevant entries before planning begins.
Strongly disagree
Strongly agree
Question 20 of 25
Each completed initiative in our organization produces at least one concrete process or system improvement — a template updated, an SOP refined, or a risk category added — that makes the next similar initiative run more effectively.

Cognitive Shift 5 · Resilience Over Recovery

From "Let's get back to normal"
to "Let's build something better"

Rate each statement based on your organization's current reality — not aspiration.

What this shift measures This is the most human — and most differentiating — of the five shifts. Task thinkers treat setbacks as disruptions to recover from. Project Thinkers treat setbacks as data points that inform the next iteration. This shift is what makes organizations genuinely resilient rather than merely operational.
Question 21 of 25
When a major initiative fails or is cancelled, our organization does not simply move on — it formally extracts what was learned, adjusts its process, and can demonstrate how that learning changed subsequent decisions.
Strongly disagree
Strongly agree
Question 22 of 25
Our leaders model resilient execution behavior — when they encounter significant setbacks, they openly acknowledge what happened, frame it as a learning opportunity, and demonstrate confidence in the path forward without minimizing the difficulty.
Strongly disagree
Strongly agree
Question 23 of 25
Our organization has a documented risk and contingency process — teams anticipate what could go wrong before it does, and have defined response plans rather than improvising when disruption occurs.
Strongly disagree
Strongly agree
Question 24 of 25
After experiencing a significant execution failure — a missed launch, a lost client, a failed initiative — our organization recovers momentum faster than it did a year ago, because we have better systems for navigating setbacks.
Strongly disagree
Strongly agree
Question 25 of 25
Our organization does not aim to simply return to where it was before a setback — we deliberately use disruption as an opportunity to improve our systems, our team, and our execution model so that we emerge from difficulty stronger than we entered it.
